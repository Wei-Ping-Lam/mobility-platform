# Extended Demo Script

Expanded walkthrough, approximately four minutes ten seconds including navigation and pauses. Timing is a guide rather than a strict limit. Speak each transition while navigating to the next view. Screen directions are not spoken. The filename is retained for continuity with the previous three-minute version.

## 0:00-0:15 | Portfolio Overview

*Screen: Open Portfolio Overview with the platform title and readiness ranking visible.*

"How can cities move thousands of event visitors more sustainably?

Mobility Readiness connects transportation evidence to practical actions, using FIFA 2026 and all eleven U.S. host cities as our case study."

## 0:15-0:35 | Readiness Chart and Heatmap

*Screen: Show the ranking and map, then scroll to What drives readiness?*

"Let's start by comparing the cities.

Readiness scores combine access, traffic management, heat safety, and venue support. Scrolling down, the heatmap reveals what drives each result. Adjustable weights let planners explore different priorities.

We combine Rice datasets, transit schedules, walking networks, parking-facility data, and cited event plans to come up with these scores."

## 0:35-0:55 | Visitor Movement > Peak Timing

*Screen: Open Visitor movement and keep both peak-timing charts visible.*

"Next, let's open Visitor Movement to see when people arrive and leave.

These curves show concentrated demand around kickoff and after the match. Shaded bands represent eighty-five to one hundred percent attendance scenarios, helping planners explore how demand changes with attendance."

## 0:55-1:10 | Visitor Movement > Transportation Mode Mix

*Screen: Select Transportation Mode Mix and show the cross-city comparison.*

"Now, switching to Mode Mix, how do visitors get there?

We estimate scheduled transit, event shuttles, private vehicles or taxis, and walking or cycling.

This comparison helps planners explore car dependence and the role of different services."

## 1:10-1:25 | Visitor Movement > Attendee Origin

*Screen: Select Attendee Origin and keep the origin breakdown visible.*

"Let's look at where those visitors come from.

Attendee Origin separates host-market, nearby U.S., long-distance U.S., and international visitors.

This helps planners consider different visitor needs, from regional connections for nearby travelers to clear transit information and wayfinding for visitors unfamiliar with the city."

## 1:25-1:55 | Venue Access

*Screen: Show the access-versus-sustainability scatterplot, then scroll to What drives access and sustainability? Finish with a brief look at the two evidence charts below.*

"Moving to Venue Access, this page brings the venue-side evidence together.

The scatterplot compares each city's access and sustainability scores. Bubble size represents venue capacity, and color shows summer temperature. An expandable section explains how both scores are calculated.

Scrolling down, the heatmap breaks out the six score inputs. The supporting charts compare transit-stop and parking-facility density, event-window frequency, and published-service evidence."

## 1:55-2:25 | Dallas > Transit Solution

*Screen: Select City action plan, choose Dallas, and open Transit solution. Pause on the recommendation, then the projected impact metrics.*

"Let's open Dallas's City Action Plan, then Transit Solution.

We recommend pre-positioning electric overflow buses before queues form, replacing conventional trips.

Below, the base scenario addresses approximately five hundred forty passengers per match, avoids one-point-two tonnes of operating CO2e, and costs roughly fourteen thousand dollars for the first event.

Vehicle-miles saved stays at zero: electrifying a trip does not eliminate it."

## 2:25-2:45 | High-Investment Alternative

*Screen: Scroll to High-investment, High-impact alternative and hold on the cost and passenger-capacity metrics.*

"Further down, let's consider a longer-term investment: electric bus rapid transit.

Here we compare estimated capital cost with potential passenger capacity, accounting for reserve vehicles and event-day delays.

This separates a near-term operating improvement from a larger investment that could serve future events and everyday travel."

## 2:45-3:00 | Traffic Controls

*Screen: Open Traffic management solution, show the documented closures in red, and briefly reveal the controls table.*

"Finally, let's switch to Traffic Management. Red lines show documented event-plan closures. The table identifies the affected locations, explains the controls, and links to their sources, bringing the operating context alongside the transit recommendations."

## 3:00-4:10 | Platform Summary and Closing

*Screen: Return to Portfolio Overview and hold on the ranking and map. Finish at the platform title. Avoid rapid navigation during the closing.*

"Returning to the portfolio, the platform connects three questions: where are the gaps, what demand should cities plan for, and which sustainable actions should they explore?

It brings cross-city comparisons, local evidence, and quantified alternatives into one workflow, helping planners move from a baseline to an actionable idea.

A few boundaries matter. Movement, origin, and mode estimates are planning scenarios, not measured visitor behavior. The timing profile is shared across cities, and attendance bands are not confidence intervals. Tournament totals count visits across matches, not unique people.

Impact figures are projected, not achieved. Emissions estimates cover operations, not full lifecycle impacts, and savings depend on actual service conditions. Costs and capital capacity require local validation; they are not quotes, funded projects, or ridership forecasts. Traffic maps show published plans, not live closures.

The next step is to validate with operators and test through pilots. Our goal is better-informed investment, lower-emission access, and improvements that last beyond one tournament."
